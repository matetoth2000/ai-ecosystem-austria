# Ecosystem Partnership Scoring for Austrian AI Web Mining

## What the literature and policy imply

The most useful way to model an **ecosystem / partnership** dimension is not as a vague “innovation” score, but as a set of typed relations among heterogeneous actors. Innovation ecosystem research emphasizes actors, activities, institutions, relations, and complementarities; entrepreneurial ecosystem research likewise highlights a distinct **relational** dimension alongside contextual and structural ones; and digital business ecosystem research treats these environments as socio-technical networks rather than isolated firms. In practice, this means a company page is valuable when it reveals *who is connected to whom, in what role, through what structure, for what purpose*. citeturn8view1turn11view0turn11view1turn12view0

For AI specifically, ecosystem participation is broader than classic R&D collaboration. AI value-chain work separates at least five layers—hardware, cloud infrastructure, data, foundation models, and applications—so meaningful web evidence can appear as upstream technology dependencies, research and testing relationships, or downstream customer deployments and use cases. Current EU AI policy also names explicit ecosystem infrastructures and intermediaries—**European Digital Innovation Hubs**, **Testing and Experimentation Facilities**, **AI Factories**, and the **AI-on-Demand platform**—as mechanisms linking firms, research actors, and adoption ecosystems. citeturn13search0turn12view1turn16view1turn16view2turn24search0turn24search10

Technology-transfer and university-industry collaboration literature points to concrete web-detectable collaboration mechanisms: joint labs, joint research projects, shared facilities, advisory boards, frameworks for partnership governance, brokerage and networking, and intermediary organisations. EU knowledge-valorisation guidance explicitly stresses networking, exchanges, advisory boards, joint infrastructures and facilities, and shared partnership frameworks; Austrian and EU programme terminology adds formal roles such as **coordinator**, **beneficiary**, **associated partner**, **scientific partner**, and **company partner**. Those labels are unusually valuable because they are already typed, explainable, and graph-ready. citeturn8view4turn12view4turn23view2turn19view0turn26view0turn8view5turn8view6turn8view9

Web-mining literature strongly supports using both **textual** and **relational** website content for ecosystem mapping, but it also gives two major cautions. First, firm website availability and structure differ by firm size, age, sector, and location, and patenting firms are overrepresented in web-mined innovation samples. Second, hyperlinks are informative but inherently ambiguous unless interpreted in context. For a scalable scorer, that argues for relation-specific query families, document-zone weighting, target-domain classes, and aggressive negative filtering rather than naive link counting. citeturn12view3turn18view0turn18view2

## Recommended ontology

Operationally, the ontology should separate **independent external ties** from **internal ownership ties**, and it should distinguish **funded participation** from **unfunded participation**. In Horizon Europe, beneficiaries receive EU funding and sign the grant agreement, while associated partners may actively implement part of the action without receiving EU funding. In Austrian FFG programmes, consortium composition is also explicitly tied to independence, and affiliated firms may count as a single group for multi-firm criteria. That makes ownership/group relations important to capture, but important to keep separate from external ecosystem edges. citeturn8view9turn15search0turn22search15turn27view0

## Multilingual lexicon and false-positive controls

The best lexicon design is **family-based and bilingual**: one keyword family per relation type, with English and German variants, page-zone boosts, and independent negative lists. In your case, “German robustness” should include compound handling and Austrian programme vocabulary, because the most informative pages will often use terms like `Kooperationsprojekt`, `Wissenschaft und Wirtschaft`, `Kompetenzzentrum`, `Konsortialführer`, `Forschungspartner`, `F&E`, or `FuE` rather than generic English startup language. The literature and policy sources also suggest that ambiguous tokens such as *partner*, *network*, *cluster*, *ecosystem*, and *platform* should rarely score strongly unless paired with a named external actor, a formal role label, or a confirming page pattern. citeturn18view0turn19view0turn27view0turn14view0

## BM25 query families

For large-scale scoring, I would not run one master “ecosystem” query. I would run **typed BM25 families** and keep separate scores per relation class. That is more scalable, more explainable, and much better for later graph construction because each family already implies a candidate edge type. The families below align well with the ecosystem literature, the web-mining evidence, and current EU/Austrian programme terminology. citeturn12view3turn18view0turn8view4turn27view0

| Query family | BM25-style core terms | Boost terms | Exclusions | Best page types |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Strategic partnership | `partner* OR partnership OR alliance OR cooperation OR Kooperation OR Partnerschaft` | `strategic OR long-term OR joint OR framework OR MoU OR co-development OR roadmap` + named organisation | social / careers / privacy / newsletter | `/partners/`, `/alliance/`, `/news/`, announcement pages |
| Research collaboration | `research OR R&D OR F&E OR FuE OR Forschungskooperation OR co-development` | `prototype OR demonstrator OR validation OR algorithm OR dataset OR publication` + named organisation | internal R&D only | `/research/`, `/innovation/`, `/projects/`, lab pages |

| Joint labs / infrastructure | `joint lab OR living lab OR competence centre OR testbed OR facility OR Infrastruktur OR Reallabor` | `shared OR together with OR operate OR establish OR pilot site OR co-location` | generic “lab/platform” branding | `/lab/`, `/center/`, `/testbed/`, `/facility/` |
| Funded project / consortium | `project OR consortium OR coordinator OR beneficiary OR project partner OR Konsortium OR Projektpartner` | `Horizon Europe OR FFG OR CORDIS OR COMET OR Christian Doppler OR EIC OR Eurostars OR Eureka OR company partner OR scientific partner` | event-only and funding-advice pages | `/projects/`, `/consortium/`, `/project/`, grant pages |

| Innovation hubs / intermediaries | `EDIH OR Digital Innovation Hub OR TEF OR AI Factory OR KIC OR innovation hub OR sandbox` | `test before invest OR experimentation OR one-stop shop OR access to finance OR skills OR AI-on-Demand` | generic “hub” branding | programme pages, hub profiles, service pages |

A practical BM25 design choice is to score these families over **zones** rather than over raw page text only. In other words, run each family against `title`, `H1/H2`, `URL slug`, `anchor text`, and `body`, and keep the zone-level evidence for explanation. A hit on `title + H1 + URL` for `funded_project_participation` should mean something very different from a weak body-only hit for the same family. That design is consistent with the fact that hyperlinks and webpage text are most useful when interpreted together and in context. citeturn12view3turn18view0

## Strong signals for meaningful ecosystem participation

The strongest ecosystem signals are not single keywords. They are **evidence bundles**: a named external actor, a typed relation phrase, a page pattern, and often an outbound link to the counterparty or project page. The more of those elements co-occur, the more meaningful the relation is likely to be for later graph construction. This follows directly from web-mining work on textual plus relational website content, hyperlink-analysis cautions about context, and the formal role vocabulary used in EU and Austrian collaboration programmes. citeturn12view3turn18view0turn8view9turn8view5turn8view6

| Signal bundle | Suggested strength | Why it matters | Use in graph construction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Named external organisation in title/H1 **and** typed relation phrase **and** outbound link to organisation/project page | Very high | Best combination of precision, interpretability, and counterparty identification | Emit typed edge immediately if negatives are absent |
| Formal project role terms such as `coordinator`, `beneficiary`, `associated partner`, `scientific partner`, `company partner` plus project/programme marker | Very high | Structured relation labels already encode participation type | Create edges to project node, funder node, and partner nodes |
| Named university/research institute plus `joint lab`, `shared facility`, `competence centre`, `research collaboration` | Very high | Strong indicator of durable knowledge-production ties | Emit `research_collaboration` or `joint_infrastructure` edge |
| Named customer plus `case study`, `use case`, `pilot`, `deployment`, `reference` | High | Strong evidence of downstream adoption and real-world application | Emit `customer_use_case` edge; attach sector/application metadata |
| Named technology provider plus `built on`, `powered by`, `integration`, `certified partner` | High | Captures upstream stack and complementor dependencies | Emit `technology_provider` or `integration_partner` edge |
| Explicit membership wording plus recognised cluster/network/hub/intermediary name | Medium to high | Good ecosystem-positioning evidence, though often lighter than a project page | Emit `member_of` edge with lower weight than formal project participation |
| Board, steering, working-group, or expert-panel roles | Medium | Governance relevance is high, but the economic/technical tie can vary | Emit governance/board edge type, separate from project/commercial edges |
| Logo wall with no explanatory text | Low to medium | Some signal, but high false-positive risk | Keep as soft evidence only; do not emit edge alone |
| Footer social links, privacy/cookie vendor lists, generic “ecosystem/platform/community” copy | Negative | Boilerplate and marketing noise dominate | Suppress or heavily downweight |

For your objective of identifying **meaningful ecosystem participation**, the strongest classes are usually these, in order: formal funded project participation; joint labs/shared infrastructures; named university or institute collaboration; named strategic partnerships with explicit scope; named customer deployments/use cases; and explicit participation in recognised intermediaries such as EDIHs, TEFs, AI Factories, EIT KIC structures, or cluster/network governance. Membership badges alone are weaker; pure social or marketing links should not count. citeturn16view1turn16view2turn16view0turn24search0turn8view4turn19view0turn27view0

The six signal families you specifically asked about should be used as follows:

| Signal source | Recommended use |
|---|---|
| **External links** | Count only links that are relation-bearing and context-bearing: links to named partner organisations, university labs, project pages, cluster/member directories, funder/project records, or formal initiative pages. Ignore footer-wide social links and legal/vendor links. |
| **Target domains** | Treat target-domain class as a prior. Academic/institute domains, project databases, funders, recognised cluster operators, EDIH/TEF/KIC/AI ecosystem sites, and named counterparties should push the score up. Social platforms and generic martech/legal vendors should push it down. |
| **Anchor text** | High value when it contains typed relation language: `partner`, `project`, `member`, `customer story`, `use case`, `consortium`, `cluster`, `EDIH`, `case study`, `scientific partner`, `company partner`. Anchor text is often more precise than body text. |
| **Organisation names mentioned in text** | Use dictionary matching or rules-based NER to detect named counterparties. Co-occurrence of a named organisation with a relation phrase inside a small text window is one of the best explainable signals. |
| **Page titles and headings** | These are your best high-precision zones. Titles such as “Projects”, “Partners”, “Research”, “Case Study”, “Reference”, “Consortium”, “Members”, “Strategic Partnerships”, “Customer Stories” should carry strong field boosts. |
| **Page URLs** | Use as page-type priors: `/partners`, `/project`, `/consortium`, `/customers`, `/references`, `/case-studies`, `/membership`, `/network`, `/cluster`, `/group`, `/labs`. URL alone should not emit edges, but it should help weight evidence. |

## Implementation guidance for scalability and explainability

For scalability, I would score **pages first**, not domains. Each page gets a per-family score, a list of matched positive terms, matched negatives, detected organisations, target-domain classes, and page-type priors. Only after that should you aggregate to company/domain level. This avoids the common failure mode where one noisy partner page causes a whole domain to look highly ecosystem-active. It also gives you a traceable explanation string for every score. citeturn12view3turn18view0

A practical architecture is:

1. **Boilerplate-aware page processing.** Remove navigation, footer, cookie banners, and sitewide partner/logo widgets when possible.
2. **Relation-family scoring.** Run the BM25 families separately, by zone.
3. **Entity linking.** Match named organisations from text, anchors, link targets, and image alt text if available.
4. **Evidence bundling.** Combine text hits, relation terms, zone weights, named entities, and outbound links into a typed evidence record.
5. **Edge emission.** Emit graph edges only when evidence crosses a family-specific threshold.
6. **Domain aggregation.** Roll up from pages to company-level relation portfolios after page scoring is complete.

For explainability, each emitted edge should keep a compact evidence bundle such as:

- `source_page_title`
- `source_url_pattern`
- `relation_family`
- `matched_en_terms`
- `matched_de_terms`
- `matched_boost_terms`
- `matched_negative_terms`
- `mentioned_organisations`
- `outbound_target_class`
- `confidence`

That is much easier to audit than a single opaque “ecosystem score”.

A simple and robust confidence logic is:

| Confidence | Minimum evidence bundle |
|---|---|
| High | Named external organisation + typed relation phrase + strong zone hit, with either outbound relation-bearing link or formal role/programme label |
| Medium | Named external organisation + typed relation phrase, but weaker page structure or no relation-bearing outbound link |
| Low | Page-type prior or logo/member evidence only, or weak body-only mentions |
| Reject | Social/legal boilerplate, internal-only pages, or high negative-term density |

Two final cautions matter for your Austrian AI mapping context. First, firm websites are systematically biased data sources: coverage and richness differ across firms, and web-mined innovation samples tend to overrepresent more visible, more innovative, and more web-active firms. Second, hyperlinks and marketing language are not self-interpreting. A good scorer therefore needs explicit negative classes, document-zone logic, and typed relation families. citeturn12view3turn18view0

My bottom-line recommendation is to make **meaningful ecosystem participation** depend mainly on these high-value edge classes:

- formal project/consortium participation,
- university and research-institute collaboration,
- joint labs/shared infrastructures/testbeds,
- explicit cluster/network/intermediary participation,
- named strategic partnerships,
- named customer deployments and technology-provider ties,

while keeping **corporate group** and **generic social/media/marketing** signals in separate channels. That design is the best fit for scalability, multilingual robustness, explainability, BM25-style scoring, and later graph construction. citeturn8view9turn27view0turn16view1turn16view2turn24search0turn19view0turn12view3
