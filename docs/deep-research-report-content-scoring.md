# Scalable Pre-LLM Page Scoring for Austrian AI Ecosystem Mapping

## What the literature implies for your problem

The core design problem is not just “relevance ranking,” but **high-recall evidence retention under severe budget constraints**. The strong theme across web-mining and innovation-measurement work is that firm websites do contain useful signals about products, services, technologies, R&D, collaboration, and organizational relationships, but those signals are mixed with large amounts of boilerplate, legal text, navigation chrome, and highly uneven site structures. Recent WebAI work frames organizational websites as a valuable but noisy source characterized by **timeliness, comprehensiveness, and relationality**, while also stressing that rigorous processing is needed to separate signal from noise. Earlier ecosystem-mapping work similarly shows that websites can support cost-effective mapping of innovation activity, cooperation, and applied technologies at scale. citeturn27view0turn6view5turn5view0

For your specific use case, the literature is unusually aligned with your goals. Studies on firm-level innovation from websites consistently find that the most useful signals are not only the raw words on pages, but also **site size, language, subpage count, and hyperlink structure**. Axenbeck and Breithaupt show that text features matter most, but that website size, English-language share, and link features add complementary predictive value; Kinne and Lenz show that prioritizing **top-level pages with short URLs** is a good way to capture general “business activity profiles” rather than drowning in low-value deep pages. These findings are directly relevant to a pre-LLM page scorer that must pick a small, high-yield subset per domain. citeturn12view1turn6view3turn33view0

The practical implication is that your scorer should be treated as a **multi-signal filter**, not a single classifier. It should combine: cleaned main text, fielded lexical evidence, URL/page-type priors, link/centrality cues, and multilingual keyword families. This is also consistent with information retrieval theory: classical probabilistic retrieval gave rise to BM25, while BM25F was specifically developed to exploit **document structure, metadata, and link-graph information**. citeturn17search2turn32view1turn32view2

## Candidate scoring approaches

A **rules-plus-priors baseline** is the cheapest and most reproducible starting point. This approach assigns scores from URL patterns, crawl depth, page language, extracted text length, heading density, and small bilingual keyword lexicons, while applying hard penalties to obvious boilerplate pages such as login, cookie, legal, and generic contact pages. This is supported by content-extraction literature showing that clean extraction materially helps downstream mining, and by firm-website work showing that top-level structure, text volume, and language are already informative before any complex modeling. citeturn23view1turn19view2turn12view1turn6view3

A **fielded lexical ranking approach** is the most natural next step. Here, each page is represented as fields such as URL slug, title, H1, H2/H3, anchor text, and body text. You then score the page against a small set of synthetic “queries” corresponding to your objectives: firm activity, AI/technology, innovation/R&D, and collaboration/ecosystem. Conceptually this is a BM25F-style design, because BM25F exists precisely to weight different fields differently, but in practice you can implement it as a weighted sum of per-field BM25 scores if you want to avoid complicated parameter tuning. This is likely the strongest **student-scale default** because BM25 is robust out of domain, cheap, and interpretable. citeturn32view1turn32view0turn6view0turn22view1

A **lightweight supervised page classifier** is worthwhile once you can label a few hundred to a few thousand pages as “useful” or “not useful.” The strongest evidence here is not that you need deep learning, but rather that **simple supervised models often go surprisingly far** on website text. Statistics Netherlands found that TF-IDF features with logistic regression performed very well for detecting innovative companies, and official-statistics work on activity classification often favors interpretable models such as Naive Bayes, SVMs, or logistic regression. Importantly, knowledge-based concept features are useful for interpretability, but one study found they did **not** outperform character n-grams for NACE prediction, which is a strong argument for including char n-grams in German/English page classifiers. citeturn7view0turn7view4turn7view5turn29view0

A **within-domain graph ranking layer** is a useful supplement, not a replacement. PageRank and HITS established that link structure carries information about authority and importance; later firm-web research extends this idea to organizational websites and inter-firm hyperlinks. For your purposes, an internal-link graph can be used to distinguish hub-like navigation pages from authority-like content pages and to reward pages that receive many substantive internal links or that sit near the homepage. However, it should remain a secondary prior because page importance and page usefulness are related, not identical. citeturn23view2turn24view0turn26search0

An **optional sparse-dense hybrid** is reasonable only after the basic system is working. Multilingual dense models such as multilingual E5, LaBSE, and BGE-M3 improve semantic and cross-lingual retrieval, and BGE-M3 is specifically designed to support multilingual retrieval and long documents. But the retrieval literature remains clear that BM25 is still a strong baseline, and dense models bring more compute, more engineering complexity, and more risk of hidden multilingual bias. For a pre-LLM retention system, the role of embeddings should be limited to a second-stage “semantic rescue” for pages that lexical methods may miss. citeturn22view3turn22view4turn22view5turn6view0turn22view1turn22view0

## Recommended scoring dimensions and features

The most defensible scoring dimensions are the ones that line up with both your research goals and established innovation-measurement frameworks. I would recommend six dimensions.

**Firm activity descriptiveness** should be the primary dimension. The scorer should prefer pages that explain what the firm does, what it sells, which sectors it serves, and how it describes its offering. This aligns with the Oslo Manual’s focus on products, services, and innovation activities, with NACE-classification work that uses website text to identify economic activity, and with Kinne and Lenz’s emphasis on general business activity profiles from top-level pages. citeturn25search3turn29view0turn33view0

**Technology and AI specificity** should be the second dimension. Pages that mention software, systems, applications, platforms, automation, machine learning, data, models, robotics, vision, NLP, or domain technologies are especially valuable for AI ecosystem mapping. This is also consistent with Axenbeck and Breithaupt, where technology-related words and topics are among the most important predictors, especially for product innovation. citeturn12view1turn6view3

**Innovation and R&D evidence** should be a separate dimension rather than being folded into general technology. The innovation literature repeatedly highlights R&D, development, intellectual property, and financing as distinct constructs. Héroux-Vaillancourt and co-authors explicitly operationalize website indicators around **R&D, IP protection, collaboration, and external financing**, while the Oslo Manual distinguishes multiple innovation activities including R&D, engineering/design, software, training, IP, and innovation management. citeturn31view0turn25search3

**Partnership and ecosystem participation** should be another explicit dimension. For ecosystem mapping, you want pages that reveal collaboration with universities, research institutes, public bodies, consortia, accelerators, clusters, customers, suppliers, and strategic partners. Official innovation frameworks and innovation-scoreboard methods both treat linkages and collaboration as central innovation capabilities, and digital-layer / WebAI work treats hyperlinks and text similarity as meaningful relational signals. citeturn25search5turn1search19turn26search0turn27view0

**Page quality and informational density** should be included as a scoring dimension, because many pages are formally “relevant” by keywords but poor evidence sources in practice. Main-content extraction work shows that text density, link density, DOM cues, and page complexity are strong indicators of extractable content, and modern benchmarks show that no single extractor dominates across all genres and complexity levels. Your scorer should therefore reward pages with coherent extracted text, informative headings, and moderate boilerplate ratios, while penalizing pages dominated by links, short snippets, or repeated template text. citeturn23view1turn21view4turn21view5

**Structural importance within the site** should be a final, lower-weight dimension. Innovative firms tend to have larger websites and different hyperlink patterns, while classic IR shows that link structure is informative about authority. This supports features such as homepage distance, shortest-URL preference, in-degree from internal links, and simple internal PageRank. citeturn6view3turn12view1turn23view2turn24view0

From these dimensions, the page-level feature set should include: URL depth and path tokens; page type inferred from slug patterns; title and headings; extracted main text length; language and language proportions; keyword-family hits; char n-grams or subword features; number and type of internal/external links; inlink count from other internal pages; simple internal PageRank/HITS-like scores; extraction confidence or complexity indicators; duplicate/near-duplicate flags; and negative boilerplate markers such as legal/contact/login/cookie text. The official-statistics and content-extraction literature gives strong precedent for all of these signals, especially text features, language, subpage counts, and network cues. citeturn12view1turn29view0turn33view0turn23view1

## How to combine URL metadata, page text, headings, and links

The cleanest practical design is a **linear or fielded ranking model** where text dominates, structure helps, and noise is penalized. A good starting principle from Axenbeck and Breithaupt is that text should carry the largest weight, because when word-based features are considered jointly, their aggregated importance is far larger than meta and network features, even though meta/network signals still add useful marginal information. citeturn12view1

A simple scoring formula can therefore look like this:

```text
FinalScore(page) =
  0.60 * FieldedTextScore
+ 0.15 * URLPageTypePrior
+ 0.15 * LinkCentralityScore
+ 0.10 * QualityAdjustment
- 0.20 * BoilerplatePenalty
```

The exact numbers are just initialization values, but the structure is important. `FieldedTextScore` should be the weighted sum of BM25-like or TF-IDF cosine scores over fields such as URL, title, H1, H2/H3, anchor text, and body. `URLPageTypePrior` should reward short, top-level URLs and path tokens like `/about`, `/solutions`, `/technology`, `/research`, `/partner`, while penalizing obvious low-value paths such as `/impressum`, `/privacy`, `/terms`, `/login`, `/cookie`, `/cart`. `LinkCentralityScore` should use simple internal in-degree or internal PageRank. `QualityAdjustment` should reward extracted text volume, heading structure, and extraction confidence. `BoilerplatePenalty` should use text/link-density cues and page-type heuristics. This combination is directly motivated by BM25F-style fielding, top-level URL prioritization, classic link analysis, and modern content extraction. citeturn32view1turn33view0turn24view0turn23view1turn19view2

If you prefer to avoid true BM25F tuning, that is perfectly reasonable. BM25F is useful conceptually, but it requires multiple field weights and normalization parameters, and the literature notes that this tuning burden can become expensive. For a student project, a **weighted sum of separate per-field BM25 scores** is easier to debug and usually sufficient. citeturn32view3turn32view4

On crawl strategy, the strongest evidence-based rule is: **start at the homepage and prioritize the shortest, top-level URLs**. Kinne and Lenz explicitly did this because general information about the firm is more likely to be available at the top level than in deep news or archival paths. They also report that site-size distributions are extremely heavy-tailed: half of firm websites can be completely scraped with a limit of 15 pages, while pushing completeness much higher requires very large page budgets because of massive outlier sites. That is a powerful argument for a quota-based scorer that searches shallow and keeps only the best pages. citeturn33view0

## German and English keyword families and method suitability

Your keyword families should be **bilingual, mirrored, and semantically grouped**, not just one long list. The innovation literature suggests at least five positive families and one negative family.

**Activity and offering**:  
English seeds such as *about, company, solutions, products, services, customers, industries, portfolio, competence, expertise*; German seeds such as *über uns, unternehmen, lösungen, produkte, dienstleistungen, kunden, branchen, portfolio, kompetenz, expertise*. These are aligned with firm-activity and business-description tasks in NACE and firm-profiling work. citeturn29view0turn33view0

**Technology and AI**:  
English seeds such as *AI, artificial intelligence, machine learning, data science, analytics, software, system, application, platform, automation, robotics, computer vision, NLP, generative AI*; German seeds such as *KI, künstliche intelligenz, maschinelles lernen, datenwissenschaft, analytik, software, system, anwendung, plattform, automatisierung, robotik, computer vision, sprachverarbeitung, generative KI*. This family is supported by the technology-related terms that appear among top predictive web features in innovation studies. citeturn12view1turn6view3

**Innovation and R&D**:  
English seeds such as *innovation, innovative, research, development, R&D, prototype, experimentation, engineering, patent, IP, intellectual property, laboratory, feasibility, roadmap*; German seeds such as *innovation, innovativ, forschung, entwicklung, F&E, prototyp, experiment, engineering, patent, geistiges eigentum, labor, machbarkeitsstudie, roadmap*. These families directly match the validated constructs used in website-based innovation indicators and Oslo Manual innovation activities. citeturn31view0turn25search3

**Collaboration and ecosystem**:  
English seeds such as *partner, partnership, collaboration, consortium, university, institute, lab, funded project, cluster, accelerator, incubator, alliance, network, member, community*; German seeds such as *partner, partnerschaft, kooperation, konsortium, universität, institut, labor, gefördertes projekt, cluster, accelerator, inkubator, allianz, netzwerk, mitglied, community*. This family is well grounded in innovation-linkage frameworks and digital-layer research. citeturn25search5turn26search0

**Market evidence and public proof**:  
English seeds such as *case study, references, customers, press release, publication, white paper, awards, certification, pilot, deployment*; German seeds such as *fallstudie, referenzen, kunden, pressemitteilung, publikation, whitepaper, auszeichnungen, zertifizierung, pilot, einführung*. These are useful because they connect claims to public evidence pages, which helps later retrieval and LLM interpretation. Innovation studies also show that firms often disclose product-oriented evidence more readily than process innovation. citeturn12view1turn15view0

**Negative or boilerplate**:  
English seeds such as *privacy, terms, cookie, legal, imprint, login, register, basket, career portal, captcha*; German seeds such as *datenschutz, agb, cookie, rechtliches, impressum, login, registrieren, warenkorb, karriereportal, captcha*. This family follows the “bloat” examples in firm-web studies and common boilerplate categories in extraction tools. citeturn33view0turn19view1turn19view2

On methods, the literature points to a very clear ordering for your case:

| Method | Best role in your system | Suitability |
|---|---|---|
| **Fielded query-family lexical scoring** | First-stage page ranking | **Highly suitable** |
| **TF-IDF** | Features for rules and simple supervised models | **Highly suitable** |
| **Char n-grams** | German/English robustness, especially for classification | **Very suitable** |
| **Linear / shallow supervised classifiers** | If you can label pages | **Very suitable** |
| **Dense multilingual embeddings** | Optional second-stage rescue or dedup | **Conditionally suitable** |
| **Deep page classifiers** | Only after you have labels and a clear failure case | **Usually not necessary at first** |

BM25 is a strong and cheap baseline in general IR and remains competitive in multilingual settings. TF-IDF is effective and interpretable in website classification. Char n-grams can outperform hand-built concept features for activity classification and are naturally robust to multilingual morphology. Multilingual embeddings are useful, but should remain optional because they add cost and multilingual retrieval can exhibit language bias. citeturn6view0turn22view1turn7view0turn29view0turn22view0turn22view3turn22view4turn22view5

## Validation and a practical student-scale architecture

The right validation target is **retention quality**, not pure page classification accuracy. In other words, the question is not whether each page is labeled perfectly, but whether the retained subset still contains the evidence needed for downstream firm mapping. Information retrieval gives you the right metrics vocabulary here: precision, recall, Precision@k, MAP, and NDCG are all standard ways to evaluate ranked results, with MAP and NDCG especially useful when rank order matters. citeturn30view1turn30view2turn30view3

A sensible validation design has three layers. First, do **page-level manual labeling** on a stratified sample of firms: label candidate pages as useful for firm description, useful for technology/AI, useful for innovation/R&D, useful for partnership/ecosystem, or not useful. Second, report **domain-level recall metrics** such as: “Does the retained top-k contain at least one firm-description page?” and “Does it contain at least one innovation/ecosystem page?” Third, run a **downstream task evaluation**: compare firm-level extraction or LLM classification using retained pages versus a higher-cost baseline, such as all shallow pages or a manually curated set. If retained-page performance is close while page count drops sharply, the scorer is doing its job. This form of downstream validation is also consistent with website-based innovation work that validates web-derived indicators against survey or official benchmarks. citeturn31view0turn7view5turn12view1

You should also validate by **language slice, sector slice, and site-size slice**. Multilingual IR work warns that retrieval can behave differently across languages, and firm-website work shows that site characteristics vary with firm size, age, and sector. That means your reported quality should be broken out at least by German-only, English-only, bilingual, and large-site-versus-small-site domains. citeturn22view0turn5view0turn27view0

A practical student-scale architecture would look like this:

Start by crawling each domain with a **strict shallow budget**, for example 20–30 pages, seeded from the homepage and ordered by shortest/top-level URLs plus sitemap candidates where available. Exclude hard redirects to other domains and obvious file downloads. This is consistent with the ARGUS strategy used in firm-web studies and with the heavy-tailed nature of firm site sizes. citeturn33view0

Then run **main-content extraction** using Trafilatura as the default extractor, with fallback to jusText or Readability when extraction is suspiciously short or highly link-heavy. This is practical because Trafilatura is actively maintained, extracts main text plus metadata, and performs strongly in benchmarks, while jusText remains a solid heuristic baseline for sentence-like content. The extraction benchmark literature also shows that performance depends on page genre and complexity, which justifies a fallback policy instead of a one-extractor-only design. citeturn19view2turn6view2turn19view3turn21view4

Next, build a **page feature store** with the following fields: normalized URL tokens, page title, H1/H2/H3, main text, anchor texts, language, internal in-degree, external-link count, simple internal PageRank, extracted-text length, link-density proxy, and page-type flags. Keep duplicates and near-duplicates under control, especially bilingual mirrors and repeated product-template pages. Text preprocessing should be conservative; notably, Kinne and Lenz report that stemming did not improve their classification performance, which is a useful warning against overprocessing. citeturn33view0

Then compute the page score in two stages. Stage one is a **hard filter** for bloat, low extracted text, and obvious non-evidence page types. Stage two is the **fielded relevance score** combining firm-description, technology/AI, innovation/R&D, and ecosystem query families. If you can label 500–1,500 pages, train a logistic regression or linear SVM on top of TF-IDF plus char n-grams and the structural features; if not, keep the ranker unsupervised and tune weights manually on a development sample. The official-statistics literature strongly supports this lightweight route, and even when deep models are available, simple shallow models remain attractive because they are interpretable, cheap, and reproducible. citeturn7view0turn7view4turn29view0

Finally, retain pages using a **diversified quota policy** rather than pure top-score truncation. For example, keep the best one or two pages from each of these buckets if present: company/about, products/services/solutions, technology/AI, research/innovation, partnership/ecosystem, and evidence pages such as news/publications/case studies. This reduces the risk that the ranking is monopolized by one template family, such as product pages or news posts, and better matches your actual downstream needs. The literature does not prescribe this exact quota rule, but it is a faithful engineering consequence of the empirical finding that websites expose **different innovation constructs on different page types**, and that product-oriented pages are often more explicit than process-oriented ones. citeturn31view0turn12view1turn15view0

The bottom-line recommendation is therefore:

Use **Trafilatura-based content extraction**, a **fielded query-family lexical scorer**, **URL and internal-link priors**, **bilingual keyword families**, and, if you can label pages, a **small logistic-regression or linear-SVM reranker over TF-IDF plus char n-grams and structure features**. Do **not** start with expensive embeddings or deep models. Add multilingual embeddings only later as a second-stage recall booster if your validation shows consistent misses on semantically relevant German/English pages that lexical methods do not capture. This is the best fit with the literature and with your constraint set: cheap, scalable, reproducible, and useful before any expensive LLM classification. citeturn19view2turn6view0turn22view1turn7view0turn29view0turn27view0
